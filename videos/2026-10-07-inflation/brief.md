# Inflation

Money 101, episode 2. Series: `money-101`.

## The idea

"Genera el segundo vídeo de la lista de reproducción." The second subject of the list of basics
the user approved on 2026-10-06 is inflation, and episode 1 announces it as it closes: "the same
snowball, rolling against the cash in your pocket."

## Verdict

**make it.** It is the question behind half of the later episodes (saving against investing,
fees, a mortgage), it is a mechanism that can be drawn, and the facts it needs are few and are on
the pages of central banks, the IMF and a statistics office. It needs no forecast: it does not
say where prices will go, only what a given rate does over time.

## The angle

Inflation is not things getting dearer one by one but money measuring less every year, a few
percent at a time and compounding: so what counts is never the number on a note, a payslip or
a savings account, but how fast that number moves compared with prices.

## Who it is for, and what they will know at the end

People in their twenties, anywhere, who have heard "inflation is at three percent" and take it
as news about shops. At the end they can say what inflation is and how it is measured, why two
percent a year is not small over a working life, what "real" means next to a pay rise or an
interest rate, why a fixed debt is the one thing inflation shrinks in the borrower's favour, and
why central banks aim for a little inflation and not for none.

## The facts

From sources (all read on 2026-10-07):

- Inflation is "a broad increase in the prices of goods and services, not just of individual
  items; it means, you can buy less for €1 today than you could yesterday." It is measured with a
  basket: in the euro area the index (HICP) "covers, on average, around 700 goods and services",
  and what households spend more on weighs more. | https://www.ecb.europa.eu/ecb-and-you/explainers/tell-me-more/html/what_is_inflation.en.html
- In the United Kingdom the statistics office checks every month "the prices of about 700 items
  in a 'basket' of goods and services"; the target is 2 percent; "Between 1997 and 2021, CPI
  inflation was 2% on average", and it "reached a peak of 11% in 2022". | https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/explainers/what-is-inflation
- The Federal Reserve judges that inflation of 2 percent over the longer run is most consistent
  with its mandate; low and stable inflation lets households and businesses plan. | https://www.federalreserve.gov/faqs/economy_14400.htm
- The European Central Bank aims for 2 percent over the medium term; the target is symmetric
  (too low is as unwelcome as too high), and aiming at 2 and not 0 leaves "a safety margin
  against the risk of deflation". | https://www.ecb.europa.eu/mopo/strategy/pricestab/html/index.en.html
- The IMF's explainer (Ceyda Oner, Back to Basics): when incomes do not rise as much as prices,
  purchasing power and real income fall, and that erosion is the biggest cost of inflation; a
  borrower with a fixed-rate mortgage at 5 percent gains from 5 percent inflation, because the
  real interest rate is zero; falling prices can lead people to delay purchases, which slows
  the economy; in 2008 Zimbabwe's inflation was estimated at one point at 500 billion percent
  a year. Told in my words: the page I could read gives these as points, not as sentences to
  quote. | https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/fandd/issues/Series/Back-to-Basics/Inflation
- United States consumer prices (CPI for All Urban Consumers, all items, not seasonally
  adjusted, 1982-84 = 100): 172.8 in August 2000 and 334.980 in August 2026, the latest month
  published. Prices are 1.94 times what they were: what cost 100 dollars costs about 194, and a
  dollar buys 52 percent of what it bought. That is 2.6 percent a year on average over 26
  years. | https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CUUR0000SA0

Arithmetic, worked out here (no source needed; the rates are examples or the central banks'
own target):

- At 2 percent a year, 100 becomes 102 after one year and 104.04 after two: the second rise is
  on the first. Prices double in 35 years (the rule of 72 from episode 1 says 72 / 2 = 36).
- What a 100 note buys at 2 percent a year: 82 after 10 years, 67 after 20, 55 after 30, 50
  after 35.
- Doubling by the rule of 72: at 3 percent, 24 years; at 10 percent, about 7.
- Pay that rises 2 percent in a year when prices rise 5 percent buys about 3 percent less
  (1.02 / 1.05 = 0.971).
- Savings that earn 3 percent while prices rise 2 percent gain about 1 percent in what they buy.
  Savings that earn 1 percent while prices rise 3 percent lose about 2 percent, though the
  balance went up.

Not to be used: the figure that prices doubled every 15 hours in Hungary in July 1946 (the
Hanke and Krus table, Cato Institute working paper, 2012). The paper's page and its PDF refuse to
be read from here, and the copies that repeat it are not the source. The OECD's release of 6
December 2022 (inflation of 10.7 percent in October 2022 across its members) could not be read
either: the video uses the Bank of England's 11 percent instead.

## The strongest objection

That "a dollar buys half of what it did in 2000" frightens more than it explains: pay rose too,
many things got better or cheaper (a phone, a flight), and the average basket is nobody's
basket. All true, and the video says each: the second half is about pay and interest keeping up
or not (the treadmill), one scene says the basket is an average and yours differs, and it does
not say that anyone is poorer, only what happens to a number that does not move. The second
objection is to the series' rule: "cash loses value" is one step from "so do not hold cash".
The video stops before that step: it shows what a rate does to a note, a payslip, a savings
account and a debt, and what "real" means, and leaves it there. Saving against investing is
episode 7.

## Outline

About 4 minutes 45 seconds, 26 scenes of 4 to 15 seconds. Figures in dollars, as the user
decided for the series. Each part has its tone of colour.

Part one, the riddle (ocean):

1. **01-drawer** (presenter). In the year 2000 a hundred-dollar bill goes into a drawer. Today
   it comes out, untouched. How much of what it could buy then can it buy now? Shows the bill
   going into a drawer.
2. **02-guess**. The bill is the same paper, the same number. Take a guess: all of it, most of
   it? Shows the bill beside a basket of ten things, and the choices.
3. **03-answer**. About half. In the United States prices have almost doubled since 2000 (BLS).
   Shows the basket emptying to five things.
4. **04-same-bill**. Nobody took anything from the drawer. The bill did not change: everything
   around it did. Shows the bill still, the price tags rising.

Part two, what it is (plum):

5. **05-inflation**. That is inflation: prices in general rising, so the same money measures
   less. Shows the word, and a ruler that gets shorter.
6. **06-title**. Title card: Inflation. Money 101, episode 2.
7. **07-one-price**. One price going up is not inflation: coffee after a bad harvest. Shows one
   boat lifted by a wave.
8. **08-tide**. Inflation is the tide: almost everything rises together. Shows the water level
   lifting every boat.
9. **09-basket**. How it is measured: every month, statisticians price a basket of about 700
   things people buy (euro area, United Kingdom). Shows the basket and its count.
10. **10-your-basket**. The basket is an average, and what you spend more on weighs more: your
    own inflation is not exactly the official one. Shows two baskets with different shares.

Part three, the snowball backwards (forest):

11. **11-two-percent**. Central banks do not aim for zero. The Fed, the ECB and the Bank of
    England all aim for 2 percent a year. Shows the three names and the figure.
12. **12-small** (presenter). Two percent sounds like nothing: 100 becomes 102. Shows that.
13. **13-compounds**. But next year's rise is on this year's prices: the snowball of episode 1.
    72 divided by 2: prices double in about 36 years. Shows 72 / 2 = 36.
14. **14-melt**. What the bill in the drawer buys at 2 percent: 82 after ten years, 67 after
    twenty, half after thirty-five. Shows an ice cube melting, step by step.
15. **15-faster**. At 3 percent, 24 years to double; at 10 percent, about 7. In 2022 inflation
    in the United Kingdom peaked at 11 percent. Shows the three rates and their years.

Part four, the treadmill (ember):

16. **16-question** (presenter). So is everybody getting poorer? No. It depends on whether what
    you have moves as fast as prices.
17. **17-treadmill**. Picture a treadmill. The belt runs backwards at the speed of prices; you
    walk at the speed of your pay. Shows the treadmill and the two speeds.
18. **18-pay**. Pay up 2 percent, prices up 5: the payslip is bigger and it buys about 3 percent
    less. Shows the walker sliding back.
19. **19-real**. That is nominal against real: the number, and what the number buys. Shows the
    two words.
20. **20-savings**. Savings at 3 percent with prices at 2: about 1 percent ahead. At 1 percent
    with prices at 3: the balance grows and buys less. Shows the two cases on the belt.

Part five, the other side and the target (indigo):

21. **21-debt**. One thing the belt carries backwards in your favour: a debt at a fixed rate.
    At 5 percent interest and 5 percent inflation, its real cost is zero (IMF). Lenders know it,
    and price it in. Shows the debt on the belt.
22. **22-why-not-zero** (presenter). Then why do central banks want 2 percent and not zero?
23. **23-deflation**. Because falling prices are worse: if things are cheaper next month,
    people wait, and the economy slows (IMF). Two percent is a margin against that (ECB).
    Shows a calendar and a purchase put off.
24. **24-too-fast**. And far too much breaks money altogether: Zimbabwe, 2008, an estimated 500
    billion percent a year (IMF). Shows the figure and a wheelbarrow of notes.
25. **25-recap**. Three things: inflation is prices in general rising; a little compounds into a
    lot; what counts is real, not the number.
26. **26-close** (presenter). Next: what a debt really costs.
