## 01-drawer

In the year two thousand, someone puts a hundred-dollar bill in a drawer, and forgets it. Today, they find it. Same bill. Same number on it. So here's the question. How much of what it could buy back then can it buy today?

## 02-guess

Say that in two thousand it filled a basket with ten things. Take a guess. Does it still buy all ten? Nine? Seven?

## 03-answer

The answer is about five. In the United States, prices have almost doubled since two thousand. So the same bill buys about half as much.

## 04-same-bill

And nobody took anything out of that drawer. The bill didn't change. Everything around it did.

## 05-inflation

That's inflation. Prices in general going up, year after year. Money is the ruler we measure everything with. And that ruler keeps shrinking.

## 06-title
seconds: 3

## 07-one-price

One price going up is not inflation. If a frost ruins the coffee harvest, coffee costs more. That's one wave, lifting one boat.

## 08-tide

Inflation is the tide. Rent. Food. Haircuts. Bus tickets. Almost everything rising together.

## 09-basket

So how do you measure a tide? With a basket. Every month, statisticians check the prices of about seven hundred things that people actually buy. If the whole basket costs two percent more than a year ago, inflation is two percent.

## 10-your-basket

But that basket is an average. Yours is your own. If rent is half of what you spend, and rent is what's rising, your inflation is higher than the official one.

## 11-two-percent

Here's the part that surprises people. Central banks don't aim for zero. The Federal Reserve. The European Central Bank. The Bank of England. All three aim for the same number. Two percent a year.

## 12-small

Two percent sounds like nothing. Something that cost a hundred dollars costs a hundred and two a year later. Who would even notice?

## 13-compounds

But next year's two percent comes on top of this year's prices. It compounds. It's the snowball from the last episode. So use the rule of seventy-two. Seventy-two divided by two. Prices double in about thirty-six years.

## 14-melt

Put the bill back in the drawer, at two percent a year. After ten years, it buys what eighty-two dollars buy today. After twenty, sixty-seven. After thirty-six, less than half. Like an ice cube. You never see it melt. You just find it smaller.

## 15-faster

And that's when everything goes to plan. At three percent, prices double in twenty-four years. At ten percent, in about seven. In twenty twenty-two, inflation in the United Kingdom peaked at eleven percent.

## 16-question

So is everybody getting poorer every year? No. Because the bill in the drawer is the only thing standing still.

## 17-treadmill

Picture a treadmill. The belt moves backwards at the speed of prices. You walk forwards at the speed of your pay. What matters is the difference.

## 18-pay

Say your pay goes up two percent, in a year when prices go up five. The number on your payslip is bigger. And it buys about three percent less. You walked forwards, and still moved back.

## 19-real

Economists have two words for this. Nominal is the number. Real is what the number buys. Most numbers you see every day are nominal. A price. A salary. A balance.

## 20-savings

It's the same with savings. Earn three percent while prices rise two, and you're about one percent ahead. Earn one percent while prices rise three, and you're about two percent behind, even though your balance grew.

## 21-debt

And there's one thing the belt carries backwards in your favor. A debt at a fixed rate. Borrow at five percent while prices rise five percent, and in real terms, that loan costs nothing. Lenders know this. So they build the inflation they expect into the rate they charge.

## 22-why-not-zero

So if inflation eats cash, why would a central bank want two percent, and not zero?

## 23-deflation

Because the opposite, deflation, is worse. When prices fall, waiting pays. People put off buying. Companies sell less. And the economy slows down. Two percent is a safety margin away from that.

## 24-too-fast

And too much breaks money altogether. In two thousand eight, inflation in Zimbabwe was estimated at five hundred billion percent a year. At that speed, money stops working as money.

## 25-recap

Three things to keep. One: inflation is prices in general rising, not just one price. Two: a small rate compounds into a big change. Three: what counts is real, not nominal. What the number buys.
