# Emergency fund

Money 101, episode 6. Series: `money-101`.

## The idea

"Quiero que vayas con el siguiente video de la playlist de youtube." The sixth subject of the
list of basics the user approved on 2026-10-06 (and kept when they cut the list to ten on
2026-10-15) is the emergency fund and what it is for. Episode 5 announces it as it closes:
"Next time: the emergency fund. What it's for, and how it works."

## Verdict

**make it.** Everyone has heard "have an emergency fund", few could say what makes one
different from any other savings, and the figures that show why it matters are published by a
central bank (the Fed's survey of households) and a statistics office (Eurostat). The mechanism
can be drawn (a spare tire that rides along unused until the day the road goes wrong), and it
needs no forecast: it says what the fund is, what happens to a surprise bill without one, how
it is measured and where it is kept.

## The angle

An emergency fund is money set aside, before anything happens, for what nobody can put on a
calendar: its job is to keep a surprise bill from turning into a debt, which is why it is
counted in months, kept safe and within reach, and meant to be used and refilled.

## Who it is for, and what they will know at the end

People in their twenties, anywhere, who have just started to budget (episode 5). At the end they
can say what an emergency fund is (in the CFPB's words, a cash reserve set aside for unplanned
expenses or financial emergencies), how it differs from the once-a-year bills of episode 5, what
a surprise bill costs when it goes on a card, why the fund is measured in months, where it is
usually kept and why it is not invested, and how it is tied to the slice left at the end of the
month.

## The facts

From sources (all read on 2026-10-17):

- Economic Well-Being of U.S. Households in 2025, Federal Reserve Board, May 2026 (survey of
  October 2025). | https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/2025-report-economic-well-being-us-households-202605.pdf
  - "When faced with a hypothetical expense of $400, 63 percent of all adults said they would have
    covered it exclusively using cash, savings, or a credit card paid off at the next statement
    (referred to, altogether, as 'cash or its equivalent')." Unchanged for several years; 68
    percent in 2021, 50 percent in 2013 (figure 24). Page 45.
  - Other ways (table 25, several answers allowed): put it on a credit card and pay it off over
    time, 15 percent; borrow from a friend or family member, 10; sell something, 7; bank loan or
    line of credit, 3; payday loan, deposit advance or overdraft, 2. "12 percent of all adults
    said they would be unable to pay the expense by any means." Page 46.
  - Largest emergency expense they could handle right now using only savings (table 26): less than
    100 dollars, 18 percent; 100 to 499, 12; 500 to 999, 9; 1,000 to 1,999, 11; 2,000 to 4,999,
    12; 5,000 or more, 38. Page 46.
  - "In 2025, 55 percent of adults said they had set aside money for three months of expenses in an
    emergency savings or 'rainy day' fund", described as "one common measure of financial
    resiliency". "Thirty percent of adults indicated they could not cover three months of
    expenses by any of these means" (borrowing, selling assets or drawing on other savings).
    Page 47.
  - "Eighty-six percent of adults who said they always had money left over at the end of the month
    said they had savings to cover three months of expenses, while 13 percent of those who never
    had money left over at the end of the month had such savings (figure 25)." Page 47.
  - "59 percent of adults had at least one type of unexpected expense in the prior 12 months". "The
    most common expenses were a major vehicle repair or replacement (30 percent of adults),
    followed by a major house or appliance repair and unexpected major medical expenses (22
    percent and 21 percent, respectively)." Their typical (median) cost: 1,000 to 1,999 dollars.
    Pages 41 and 42.
- An essential guide to building an emergency fund, Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (last
  modified 29 October 2025). | https://www.consumerfinance.gov/an-essential-guide-to-building-an-emergency-fund/
  - "An emergency fund is a cash reserve that's specifically set aside for unplanned expenses or
    financial emergencies. Some common examples include car repairs, home repairs, medical bills,
    or a loss of income."
  - With a credit card or a loan, the one-time emergency expense can grow much larger than the
    original bill because of interest and fees.
  - How much: "The amount you need to have in an emergency savings fund depends on your
    situation"; think about the unexpected expenses you have had in the past and what they cost.
    No number of months is given.
  - Where: "safe, accessible, and in a place where you're not tempted to spend it on
    non-emergencies"; a bank or credit union account is "generally considered one of the safest
    places to put your money". If it is spent down, work to build it up again.

Arithmetic, worked out here:

- A 1,000-dollar repair on a card at 22 percent a year (1.83 percent a month), paid off in
  twelve equal monthly payments: about 93.59 dollars a month, 1,123 in all, so about 123 dollars
  of interest ("about a hundred and twenty dollars").

Not used: the "three to six months of expenses" rule of thumb, which would tell the viewer what
size their fund should be; the video uses three months only as the Fed's measure and quotes the
CFPB, which gives no number. Interest rates on savings accounts, which change and would invite a
comparison of banks.

## The strongest objection

That "have an emergency fund" is advice. The video says what a fund is, what the two agencies
measure and what happens to a bill without one; it never says how big anyone's fund should be,
and quotes the CFPB saying it depends on the person. The second objection is that many people
cannot save at all: the video says so with the Fed's figures (30 percent could not cover three
months by any means; 13 percent of those who never have money left over have that cushion). The
third is that the riddle is about Americans: the EU's figure comes right after, from Eurostat.

Part three, without one (ember):

14. **14-math**. A 1,000-dollar repair over twelve payments: about 120 dollars of interest.

Part four, how it works (forest):

16. **16-months**. Measured in months, not dollars: months fit any life.
17. **17-three-months**. The Fed's measure, three months: 55 percent have it; 30 percent could not

18. **18-ladder**. The largest emergency payable from savings: 18 percent under 100 dollars, 38

19. **19-eu**. Eurostat: almost three in ten could not pay an unexpected necessary expense from

20. **20-how-much**. The CFPB gives no number: it depends; look back at your past surprises.
21. **21-where**. Safe, accessible, away from temptation; a bank or credit union account.
22. **22-not-invested**. Not invested: it could be smaller on the day it is needed, a spare that

23. **23-refill**. Used is not failed: the spare goes on, then a new one. Refilled a little at a

24. **24-leftover**. Back to the pizza: 86 percent of those who always have money left have three

Part five, the recap (indigo):
