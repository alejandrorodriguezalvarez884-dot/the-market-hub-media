## 01-bill

A surprise bill. Four hundred dollars. The car needs a repair, or the phone dies. So here's a question. Out of every ten adults in the United States, how many would pay it with cash, or something just as good?

## 02-guess

Take a guess. Pause it if you like. Three out of ten? Five? Or eight?

## 03-answer

About six. Sixty-three percent would pay it with cash, savings, or a card they clear at the next bill.

## 04-others

The rest would put it on a card and pay it off over time. Or borrow from family. Or sell something. And twelve percent said they couldn't pay it at all.

## 05-gap

Same bill. Very different months. And the difference isn't the bill. It's whether some money was already waiting for it. That money is an emergency fund.

## 07-spare

Think of the spare tire in a car. Nobody buys it to use it. It just rides along, month after month. Until the day the road goes wrong. Then it's the only thing that matters.

## 08-defined

The consumer protection bureau in the United States defines it simply. A cash reserve, set aside for unplanned expenses or financial emergencies. Two words carry it. Set aside. And unplanned.

## 09-examples

Its examples are ordinary. A car repair. A home repair. A medical bill. Or losing your income.

## 10-not-rare

And they're not rare. In one year, fifty-nine percent of American adults had at least one big unexpected expense. The most common was a car repair or replacement. Thirty percent.

## 11-not-yearly

Remember the bills that come once a year, from last episode? Insurance. Presents. Those aren't emergencies. You know they're coming, so they get their own slice. The emergency fund is for what nobody can put on a calendar.

## 12-without

Now take the spare tire away. A flat stops being an hour by the side of the road. It becomes a tow truck. A surprise bill works the same way. Without money set aside, it has to go somewhere else.

## 13-card

Very often, onto a credit card. In episode three we saw the rate. On cards that charged interest, the average was about twenty-two percent a year.

## 14-math

Say a thousand-dollar repair goes on that card. It's paid off in twelve equal monthly payments. The interest adds about a hundred and twenty dollars. The repair cost more than the repair.

## 15-debt

The consumer bureau puts it this way. Pay for an emergency with a card or a loan, and a one-time expense can grow much larger than the original bill. That's the fund's real job. It keeps a surprise from turning into a debt.

## 16-months

So how big is one? It's often measured in months, not in dollars. How many months of your spending it could carry if your pay stopped. A month costs very different amounts for different lives. Months fit any of them.

## 17-three-months

The Federal Reserve uses three months as a common measure. In 2025, fifty-five percent of American adults said they had that much set aside. Thirty percent couldn't cover three months by any means, not even by borrowing.

## 18-ladder

They were also asked about the biggest emergency they could pay from savings right now. Eighteen percent said less than a hundred dollars. Thirty-eight percent said five thousand or more.

## 19-eu

In the European Union, the question is whether a household could pay an unexpected, necessary expense from its own money. In 2025, almost three in ten couldn't. From about fifteen percent in the Netherlands, to about half in Greece.

## 20-how-much

So what size is right? The consumer bureau gives no number. It says it depends on your situation. And it suggests looking back at the surprises you've had, and what they cost.

## 21-where

Where does it live? The bureau's three words are safe, accessible, and away from temptation. Somewhere it won't get spent on everyday things. A bank or credit union account is the usual home.

## 22-not-invested

Why not invest it, and let it grow? Because something that can fall in value might be smaller on the very day you need it. That's a spare tire that might be flat. The trade between growth and risk is the next episode.

## 23-refill

And when the fund gets used, that's not a failure. That's its job. The spare goes on, and then you get a new one. The fund gets refilled, a little at a time.

## 24-leftover

That's where last episode's pizza comes back. Of American adults who always had money left at the end of the month, eighty-six percent had three months set aside. Of those who never did, thirteen percent.

## 25-recap

Three things to keep. One: an emergency fund is money set aside for what nobody can plan. Two: without it, a surprise bill tends to turn into a debt. Three: it's counted in months, kept safe and easy to reach, and it's there to be used, and refilled.
