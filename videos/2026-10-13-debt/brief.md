# Debt

Money 101, episode 3. Series: `money-101`.

## The idea

"Haz el siguiente vídeo de la lista y súbelo." The third subject of the list of basics the user
approved on 2026-10-06 is what a debt really costs (the APR, the minimum payment of a card), and
episode 2 announces it as it closes: "Next time: debt. What borrowing really costs, once the
snowball rolls against you."

## Verdict

**make it.** The question is one almost anyone with a card has had and never worked out, the
mechanism can be drawn (a tub, a tap and a bucket), and the facts are few and are in a central
bank's release, a regulator's report and the rule that writes the warning on a card statement.
It needs no forecast: it says what a rate and a way of paying do over time.

## The angle

What a debt costs is not its rate alone but the rate times the time it stays owed, and a
card's minimum payment is the way of paying that keeps it owed the longest: it shrinks as the
debt shrinks, so a balance paid "on time" for eleven years costs more in interest than the thing
that was bought.

## Who it is for, and what they will know at the end

People in their twenties, anywhere, who have or will have a card or a loan, and who read "22%
APR" as a detail and "minimum payment" as what the bill costs. At the end they can say what an
APR is and what it is for, why the same debt costs so differently from one kind of loan to
another, how a card works out its minimum and why paying it takes years, what changes when the
payment does not shrink, and what the warning box on a statement means.

Arithmetic, worked out here (no source needed: the rate is an example near the Federal
Reserve's average, and the way of working out the minimum is the one the CFPB's report describes).
Interest is taken as a twelfth of the yearly rate each month, on what is owed; a card that
counts it by the day comes out a little dearer. The same sums, with the CFPB's own example
(2,000 dollars, 29 percent, a floor of 35), give 119 months and 3,134 dollars of interest: its
"almost 10-year period" and "over $3,000".

- 3,000 dollars at 22 percent a year: 55 dollars of interest in the first month, 660 in a year
  if nothing comes off.
- Paying the minimum (the month's interest plus 1 percent of what is owed, and never less than
  40 dollars), with nothing new bought: the first bill is 85 dollars, 55 of interest and 30 off
  the debt. After a year the bill is 76 dollars; after five years, 47, and 1,641 dollars are
  still owed. It ends after 133 months, a little over eleven years, with 3,793 dollars of
  interest paid: 6,793 in all.
- Paying 85 dollars every month instead: 58 months (under five years), 1,873 of interest.
- Paying 150 every month: 26 months, 771 of interest.

Not to be used: the FCA's "around £2.50 for every £1 repaid" (the same release) describes the
customers it studied in 2017, and its 3.3 million is of that year too: old figures for a video
of today. The 27.5 percent average APR of cards opened in 2024 (CFPB, page 5) is left out so the
video has one rate and one source for it.

## The strongest objection

That the example is a scare: most people do not pay only the minimum (the CFPB's own figure is
15 percent), 3,000 dollars is one balance among many, and for some the minimum is what they can
pay that month. All true, and the video says the first and does not judge the last: it shows
what a way of paying does to a debt, not what anyone ought to pay. The second objection is that
it makes borrowing sound bad. One scene says it plainly: borrowing is how people get a home
before they have saved for it; what is paid for is the time. The third is to the series' rule:
"paying more costs less" is one step from advice. The video stops before the step. It compares
three ways of paying the same debt and gives the three results, and it describes the rule that a
balance paid in full by the due date pays no interest on purchases. It never says which to
choose.

One thing to get right: episode 2 promised "once the snowball rolls against you". With a
minimum payment interest does not compound, because each bill pays the month's interest in
full; the cost comes from the years, not from interest on interest. So the video does not call
this a snowball. It says that in episode 1 time worked for you, and here time is what you pay
for.
