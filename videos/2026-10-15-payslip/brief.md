# Payslip

Money 101, episode 4. Series: `money-101`.

## The idea

"Quiero que subas un nuevo video de la serie de money 101 a youtube y a instagram." The fourth
subject of the list of basics the user approved on 2026-10-06 is where a payslip goes, gross and
net, and episode 3 announces it as it closes: "Next time: your payslip. And where the money goes
between what you earn and what you actually get."

## Verdict

**make it.** Almost everyone who works has looked at a payslip and not known what the lines
mean, and almost everyone has heard that a raise into a higher bracket can leave you with less.
The mechanism can be drawn (a row of glasses that fill in order), the facts come from one
international report (the OECD's Taxing Wages 2026) and two tax authorities, and it needs no
forecast: it says how a payslip is built and what a raise does to it.

## The angle

Between gross and net pay there are two things, income tax and social contributions, and the
income tax is charged in layers: a higher rate only touches the dollars above its line, so a
raise into a higher bracket never takes away the pay you already had. The marginal rate (what
the next dollar pays) and the average rate (all the tax over all the pay) are different numbers,
and confusing them is where the myth comes from.

## Who it is for, and what they will know at the end

People in their twenties, anywhere, with a first or second job, who read the number on the offer
as their pay. At the end they can say what gross and net are, what the two big deductions are
for, how brackets work, the difference between a marginal and an average rate, that the employer
pays contributions of its own that never reach the payslip, and how much the gap is across the
OECD and from one country to another.

## The facts

From sources (all read on 2026-10-15):

- Taxing Wages 2026, OECD, press release of 22 April 2026: for a single worker earning the
  average wage, the tax wedge "ranged from 0% in Colombia to 52.5% in Belgium in 2025" and
  "averaged 35.1% of labour costs". The tax wedge "shows total taxes on labour paid by employees
  and employers, minus cash benefits received by working families, as a percentage of labour
  costs". | https://www.oecd.org/en/about/news/press-releases/2026/04/taxes-on-labour-continue-to-rise-across-oecd-countries.html
- Taxing Wages 2026, chapter 3, "Effective tax rates on labour income in 2025": | https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/taxing-wages-2026_3a5169ef-en/full-report/effective-tax-rates-on-labour-income-in-2025_b7bb1854.html
  - Labour costs are "defined as the gross wage plus employer SSCs (including payroll taxes)";
    "labour costs are significantly higher than gross wages, because any employer SSCs
    (including payroll taxes) are taken into account".
  - Single worker at the average wage, tax wedge: "zero in Colombia and 7.5% in Chile to 49.3%
    in Germany and 52.5% in Belgium" (Table 3.1: OECD average 35.1).
  - Single worker at the average wage, as a percentage of gross wage earnings (Tables 3.2, 3.4,
    3.5): income tax plus employee contributions, OECD average 25.1 percent; income tax alone,
    15.5; employee contributions alone, 9.6.
  - "personal income tax and employee SSCs absorb between 25% and 55% of a worker's pay rise for
    single individuals without children at the average wage level in most countries."
  - On marginal rates that climb: "These higher marginal rates arise because of the phase-out of
    income-tested tax reliefs and/or cash benefits. When an income-tested measure is phased out,
    the reduction in the relief or benefit compounds the increase in the tax payable."
  - The lower-paid single worker "faced a lower tax burden in all countries except in Colombia,
    Estonia and Hungary in 2025" (Estonia and Hungary apply a flat rate).
- Social security contributions, OECD indicator: "all compulsory payments to general government
  that confer an entitlement to receive a future social benefit", among them "unemployment
  insurance benefits", "sickness benefits", "old-age, disability and survivors' pensions",
  "reimbursements for medical and hospital expenses". "Contributions may be levied on both
  employees and employers." | https://www.oecd.org/en/data/indicators/social-security-contributions.html
- Federal income tax rates and brackets, IRS: "You pay tax as a percentage of your income in
  layers called tax brackets." "When your income jumps to a higher tax bracket, you don't pay
  the higher rate on your entire income." "You pay the higher rate only on the part that's in
  the new tax bracket." | https://www.irs.gov/filing/federal-income-tax-rates-and-brackets
- Topic no. 751, Social Security and Medicare withholding rates, IRS: "The current tax rate for
  Social Security is 6.2% for the employer and 6.2% for the employee"; "The current rate for
  Medicare is 1.45% for the employer and 1.45% for the employee". | https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc751
- Income Tax rates and Personal Allowances, GOV.UK, tax year 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027:
  Personal Allowance up to 12,570 pounds at 0 percent; basic rate 20 percent from 12,571 to
  50,270; higher rate 40 percent from 50,271 to 125,140; additional rate 45 percent above.
  | https://www.gov.uk/income-tax-rates

Arithmetic, worked out here, with an example tax that is no country's (said on screen): the
first 10,000 dollars a year at 0 percent, 10,000 to 40,000 at 20 percent, above 40,000 at 40
percent. Income tax only, to keep the sums to one kind of deduction.

- On 40,000: 0 + 20 percent of 30,000 = 6,000 of tax; 34,000 left. Average rate 15 percent;
  marginal rate 20 percent until the line, 40 above it.
- A raise of 1,000, to 41,000: the 1,000 is all above the line, 40 percent of it is 400; tax
  6,400; 34,600 left. 600 more than before. The average rate goes from 15 to about 15.6 percent;
  the marginal rate is 40.
- Out of every 100 dollars of gross pay of the OECD's average single worker: about 15 to income
  tax, about 10 to contributions, about 75 arrive (Tables 3.4, 3.5, 3.2, rounded).
- In the United States, Social Security and Medicare: 6.2 plus 1.45 is 7.65 percent from the
  worker, and the same again from the employer.

Not used: the claim, common in personal-finance blogs, that "nearly half of Americans" believe a
raise can lower their take-home pay: no primary survey was found behind it. Colombia's zero
wedge is left out of the range on screen because the report explains it is a matter of
classification (its contributions are paid to funds outside government and not counted); the
video uses Chile, Germany and Belgium.

## The strongest objection

That the riddle is too clean: a raise can leave someone with less, when it costs them a benefit
or a tax credit that stops or shrinks with income. True, and the video says so in its own scene,
with the OECD's sentence behind it: that comes from the benefit, not from the bracket. The
second objection is that a made-up tax is not real. The video says it is an example, and then
shows a real one with the same shape (the United Kingdom's this year). The third is to the
series' rule: "a raise is worth taking" is one step from advice. The video never says it; it
shows what a raise does to the arithmetic and stops there. It also takes no side on whether
taxes are too high or too low: it says what each deduction pays for, in the OECD's terms.

## Outline

About 5 minutes, 27 scenes of 4 to 15 seconds. Figures in dollars, as the user decided for the
series; the real figures are the OECD's, with one each from the United States and the United
Kingdom. Each part has its tone of colour.

Part one, the riddle (ocean):

1. **01-raise** (presenter). A raise of a thousand dollars that moves you into a higher bracket,
   and a friend who warns you might take home less. Do you? Shows the raise and the warning.
2. **02-guess**. The new bracket charges forty percent instead of twenty. More, less or the same?
   Shows the two rates and the three choices.
3. **03-answer**. More: about six hundred of the thousand. The higher rate takes four hundred,
   and nothing of what you had. Shows the thousand split in two.
4. **04-line**. Because the higher rate only touches the part above the line. Shows a pay bar
   with a line on it and only the part above turning colour.
5. **05-irs**. The United States' tax office says it in one line (IRS, quoted). Shows the quote.

Part two, gross and net (plum):

6. **06-title**. Title card: Your payslip. Money 101, episode 4.
7. **07-two-numbers**. Gross pay, on paper, and net pay, what arrives. Shows the two numbers.
8. **08-receipt**. The payslip is the receipt between them; the difference goes mostly two
   places. Shows a payslip drawn with its lines.
9. **09-income-tax**. Income tax: to the government, for what the state runs. Shows it.
10. **10-contributions**. Social contributions: a right to something later (pension, sick pay,
    unemployment, often health care; OECD). Shows four small drawings.
11. **11-quarter**. Across the OECD, the average single worker gave up about a quarter of gross
    pay in 2025: about 15 in income tax, about 10 in contributions, out of 100. Shows 100 dots.

Part three, the glasses (forest):

12. **12-glasses**. How does income tax decide how much? A row of glasses. Shows three glasses.
13. **13-pour**. Pay poured in from the left fills the first, then spills to the second and the
    third. Shows the pouring.
14. **14-rates**. Each glass has its rate: an example, 0 on the first 10,000, 20 up to 40,000,
    40 above. Shows the three rates on the glasses.
15. **15-forty**. On 40,000: the second glass holds 30,000 and pays 20 percent: 6,000 in all.
16. **16-raise**. The raise: 1,000 more, all in the third glass: 400. The first two do not
    change. Shows the third glass filling a little.
17. **17-two-rates**. Marginal rate (the next dollar: 40) and average rate (all the tax over all
    the pay: 15). Shows the two side by side.
18. **18-real**. A real one, the same shape: the United Kingdom this year, 0, 20 and 40 percent
    (GOV.UK). Shows its three glasses.

Part four, what the payslip does not show (ember):

19. **19-hidden** (presenter). One more slice, and it is not on the payslip at all.
20. **20-employer**. The employer pays contributions too: in the United States, 7.65 percent
    from you and the same again from the employer (IRS). Shows the two halves.
21. **21-cost**. So a job costs more than its gross pay: the labour cost (OECD). Shows a stack:
    gross, and the employer's part on top.
22. **22-wedge**. The gap between the cost and what arrives is the tax wedge: about 35 of every
    100 an employer spent, on the average wage, across the OECD in 2025. Shows it.
23. **23-countries**. Under 8 percent in Chile, about 49 in Germany, over 52 in Belgium. Shows

24. **24-raise-real**. Of a real raise, income tax and contributions took between a quarter and
    just over half, in most OECD countries (single worker, average wage). Shows the range.
25. **25-catch**. The real catch is not the bracket: benefits and credits that shrink as pay
    grows (OECD). The bracket itself never takes what you had.

Part five, the recap (indigo):

26. **26-recap** (presenter). Three things: gross is paid, net arrives, the payslip shows the
    gap; the gap is mostly income tax and contributions, and the employer pays some you never

27. **27-close** (presenter). Next: a budget, not as a diet but as a way of sharing out what

## The Short

Scenes 01-raise, 02-guess, 03-answer, 04-line and 05-irs: the riddle of the raise into a higher
bracket. About 50 seconds, one whole idea (a higher bracket only taxes the dollars above its
line, so the raise leaves you with more), and it needs nothing from the scenes left out. It is
the same cut as the earlier episodes, the riddle, so the Shorts of the series look alike.

## Open questions for the user

None that stop it. The user asked for a new video of the series and for it to be uploaded to
YouTube and Instagram, so the brief did not go to them before the script. Decided here,
following what they decided for the earlier episodes and their wish not to be asked small
things:

- The bracket example is a made-up tax with round numbers (0, 20, 40 percent; lines at 10,000
  and 40,000 dollars), said on screen to be an example, followed by a real one with the same
  shape: the United Kingdom's, which has exactly those three rates.
- The international figures are the OECD's single worker without children on the average wage,
  the report's headline case; families are left out (their benefits would take a scene of
  their own).
- Pension savings and health insurance that some employers take from pay before it arrives are
  left out: they differ from one country and one job to another, and the video keeps to the two
  deductions every country in the report has in some form.
