## 01-raise

Your boss offers you a raise. A thousand dollars a year. But it pushes you into a higher tax bracket. And a friend warns you. Careful. You might end up taking home less. So here's the question. Would you?

## 02-guess

Say the new bracket charges forty percent, instead of twenty. Take a guess. More money? Less? Or the same?

## 03-answer

You take home more. About six hundred dollars more, out of the thousand. The higher rate takes four hundred. And not a cent of the pay you already had.

## 04-line

Because the higher rate only touches the part above the line. Every dollar below it is taxed exactly as before. The raise can't drag the rest of your pay up with it.

## 05-irs

The tax office in the United States puts it in one line. You pay the higher rate only on the part that's in the new tax bracket.

## 07-two-numbers

Every job has two numbers. Gross pay. That's what you're paid on paper. And net pay. That's what actually lands in your account.

## 08-receipt

The payslip is the receipt between the two. Line by line, it says where the difference went. And mostly, it went to two places.

## 09-income-tax

The first is income tax. It goes to the government. And it pays for what the state runs. Roads. Schools. Courts.

## 10-contributions

The second is social contributions. They buy you a right to something later. A pension when you're old. Pay when you're sick. Benefits if you lose your job. And often, health care.

## 11-quarter

So how much goes? Across the thirty-eight countries of the OECD, a single worker on the average wage gave up about a quarter of gross pay in twenty twenty-five. About fifteen percent in income tax. And about ten in contributions.

## 12-glasses

Now, back to the riddle. How does income tax decide how much to take? Picture a row of glasses.

## 13-pour

Your pay is poured in from the left. It fills the first glass. Then it spills into the second. Then into the third.

## 14-rates

Each glass has its own rate. In this example, the first ten thousand dollars pay nothing. Everything up to forty thousand pays twenty percent. And everything above that pays forty. It's a made-up tax, to keep the sums simple.

## 15-forty

So on forty thousand dollars a year, the first glass pays nothing. The second glass holds thirty thousand, and pays twenty percent of it. That's six thousand dollars in all.

## 16-raise

Now the raise. One thousand dollars more. Where does it go? All of it lands in the third glass. Forty percent of it is four hundred. The first two glasses don't change at all.

## 17-two-rates

So there are two rates in your life. The marginal rate is what your next dollar pays. Here, forty percent. The average rate is all the tax, divided by all the pay. Here, fifteen percent.

## 18-real

Real taxes have the same shape. In the United Kingdom this year, the first twelve and a half thousand pounds pay no income tax. Then twenty percent, up to about fifty thousand. Then forty.

## 19-hidden

There's one more slice. And it isn't on your payslip at all.

## 20-employer

In many countries, your employer pays contributions too, on top of your gross pay. In the United States, Social Security and Medicare take seven point six five percent of your pay. And your employer pays the same again.

## 21-cost

So a job costs more than its gross pay. What the employer spends is your gross pay, plus its own contributions. Economists call that the labour cost.

## 22-wedge

And the gap between that cost and what you take home has a name. The tax wedge. Across the OECD, on the average wage, it was about thirty-five dollars of every hundred the employer spent.

## 23-countries

But it depends a lot on where you work. In Chile, the wedge was under eight percent. In Germany, about forty-nine. In Belgium, over fifty-two. Same glasses. Very different rates.

## 24-raise-real

So what does a real raise lose? For a single worker on the average wage, income tax and contributions took between a quarter and just over half of it. That's in most OECD countries.

## 25-catch

There is one real catch, and it isn't the bracket. Some benefits and tax credits shrink as you earn more. Then a raise loses a bit of those too, and the marginal rate climbs. The bracket on its own never takes back the pay you had.

## 26-recap

Three things to keep. One: gross is what you're paid, net is what arrives, and the payslip shows the gap. Two: the gap is mostly income tax and contributions. And your employer pays some you never see. Three: a higher bracket only taxes the dollars above its line.
